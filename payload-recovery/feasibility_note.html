Can a satellite find a 70 cm white sphere in a Spanish field?

Short answer: yes — comfortably at 30 cm, marginally at 50 cm. But the hard part was never seeing it. It is telling it apart from every other white blob in the Meseta.

The target

A sphere is not a white panel

The instinct is to treat a 70 cm white porexpan ball as a 0.385 m² patch of albedo 0.80. It is not. A Lambertian sphere scatters flux across a curved surface, and only part of that curvature is simultaneously lit and facing the sensor. Integrating the lit-and-visible cap gives an apparent reflectance over the sphere's projected footprint of

ρeff
0.53 – 0.59   (sun high → sun low)
vs. flat panel
67% – 73% of the material albedo
Footprint
0.385 m²  =  4.3 px at 30 cm, 1.5 px at 50 cm

So you lose about a quarter of the brightness you might have budgeted for. It still does not matter much — against dry Meseta soil at ρ ≈ 0.20 the surface contrast is roughly 0.39 reflectance units, and about 0.24 after atmospheric attenuation. That is an enormous signal. Sensor noise is nowhere near the limiting factor: Pléiades Neo's noise-equivalent contrast is around 0.0013, some 180× smaller than the signal.

The shadow is the bigger target

A sphere sitting on soil stands 0.70 m proud of the ground, and for most of the year in central Spain its shadow presents more pixels than the sphere itself. At a typical 10:30 local-solar overpass at latitude 40.4°N:

Operational consequence

Prefer a low sun elevation acquisition, not a high one. The usual instinct is to want the brightest, most overhead illumination; here the opposite is true. A low sun roughly triples the signature and adds a directional bright–dark dipole that is far more distinctive than a bright dot alone.

In practice you cannot choose: the 30 cm optical constellations fly sun-synchronous orbits with a mid-morning descending node, so sun elevation is set by your date, not by your order. For an August landing imaged promptly, that means about 57° and a 0.46 m shadow — the weakest geometry of the year.

This flight

An August landing is the hard case, and it is on a clock

Recomputing for the actual conditions — mid-August, sun at 57°, and post-harvest cereal stubble at ρ ≈ 0.30, which is the brightest realistic background — costs you on both sides at once. The shadow shrinks to 2.8 pixels and the stubble narrows the contrast:

Pléiades Neo absorbs this comfortably — CCR 11 is still a clean detection, because the sphere's own four pixels carry the signature even when the shadow is weak. The 50 cm options do not absorb it as well: they fall to around 5.5, where the sphere is a one-and-a-half pixel blob with a one-pixel shadow. That is still a detection, but it is no longer a description, and description is what rejects bales. In August the case for 30 cm over 50 cm is stronger than it would be in winter, not weaker.

Time pressure

Cereal harvest across the Meseta finishes through July, and stubble ploughing runs from August into September. A sphere lying in a stubble field has a genuinely limited window before it is buried, shredded, or pushed into a field margin. This argues for accepting a good-enough acquisition now over an optimal one later, and it is worth saying plainly to both providers — a deadline that is real makes a goodwill request easier to prioritise, not harder.

Sensor selection

Two things to take from this. First, Pléiades Neo is the right instrument — at 30 cm the sphere is a genuine multi-pixel object with a resolvable shadow, not a single hot pixel, and its six bands give you a little spectral leverage on top. Second, PlanetScope will not find it. At 3 m the sphere fills 4% of a pixel and lifts it by 0.008 in reflectance, which is below the day-to-day radiometric and co-registration stability of the archive. That is worth stating plainly, because the daily-revisit archive is the tempting free option and it will only waste time as a detector.

PlanetScope is still worth pulling, just for a different job: masking the search area down to plausible terrain, confirming which fields were harvested or ploughed between the flight and the acquisition, and checking cloud state on candidate dates. Use it to shrink and schedule, not to detect.

The actual problem

Discrimination, not detection

Central Spain is full of bright compact objects around a metre across: white-wrapped silage bales, limestone float, plastic sheeting, agricultural debris, and a great many sheep. A single image gives you thousands of candidates in a 50 km² box. The filters that actually work are temporal.

The archive baseline is what turns this from impossible into a morning's work. Anything already present before the flight — every rock, every permanent structure — subtracts away. Two post-landing dates then kill everything that moves. What survives is a short list of small, bright, sun-aligned dipoles that appeared in your window and stayed put, which is a very specific description of your payload.

The one that bites

Round silage bales are the residual problem: white, compact, three-dimensional, they cast a shadow, and they appear in bursts during baling season. Your discriminator is size — a bale is 1.2–1.5 m against your 0.70 m — and that discrimination only exists at 30 cm. At 50 cm a bale and a sphere are both “a bright blob about two pixels across”. This is the single strongest argument for Pléiades Neo over SkySat.

Worth knowing before the conversation: SkySat tasking carries an order minimum around $15,000 and archive around $5,000, with a 25 km² polygon minimum. Pléiades Neo tasking has a practical minimum near 100 km², dropping to about 50 km² under a subscription. Your cost is set by the order minimum, not by your area. A 10 km² box and a 100 km² box cost the same, which means there is no financial reason to over-tighten the AOI — but there is every analytical reason to, since candidate count scales linearly with area.

This is also precisely why the goodwill route is worth pursuing. The marginal cost to Planet or Airbus of one pass over an existing collection corridor is small; the invoice minimum is a commercial floor, not a physical one.

Sequence

How to run the search

  1. Reconstruct the descent before buying anything

    Last telemetry fix, burst altitude, descent rate, and ERA5 winds at the actual flight time, integrated down through the column. Free, and the highest-leverage step available: halving the ellipse halves the candidate list. Tooling for this is written and tested — it takes a telemetry CSV and emits the 50% and 95% ellipses plus a square tasking AOI as GeoJSON. If reanalysis is unavailable it derives the wind profile from the balloon's own ascent track, which is arguably better: it is the actual air the payload flew through.

  2. Check whether someone already photographed it

    Spain's PNOA orthophoto programme flies at 25–50 cm and regional flights over Castilla y León and Castilla-La Mancha are frequent. If a flight passed over the area after the landing date, that imagery is free and already at the resolution you need. Check the IGN archive before spending anything.

  3. Mask the search area

    Use Sentinel-2 and PlanetScope to strip out water, woodland canopy, urban fabric and anything under structure. You are not detecting with these; you are deciding where a white sphere could plausibly be visible from above at all.

  4. Pull the archive baseline

    Latest pre-flight high-resolution coverage of the masked area. Without this the whole approach degrades by an order of magnitude, so secure it before committing to tasking.

  5. Task two post-landing acquisitions

    Separated by at least three days so that anything mobile decorrelates. Low sun elevation preferred.

  6. Run a matched filter, not a threshold

    The template is a bright disc of 0.7 m with a dark ellipse trailing along the known solar azimuth at the known length for that date and time. Both lobes, correct orientation, correct separation, scored in sigma units and stacked with cross-date persistence and novelty against the baseline. This is written and validated: on a synthetic 30 cm scene seeded with 73 confusers — bales, limestone float, sheep, and bales appearing after the baseline — the target ranked first at 22.8σ against a next-best of 3.6.

  7. Ground-truth the shortlist

    A hundred-odd candidates is a day of driving, or an afternoon of drone sorties. Sort by descending match score and by accessibility, and go look.

Worth weighing honestly

If the ellipse is genuinely small — say under 20 km² — a contracted VTOL drone survey at 3 cm ground sampling would find the sphere with near-certainty in two or three days for roughly €1,800, and it sees under partial canopy and into ditches where a satellite never will. The satellite route earns its place when the ellipse is large, when access is awkward, or when the imagery arrives as a favour rather than an invoice. The two combine well: satellite to triage a wide area down to a shortlist, drone to close it out.

Make the payload answer back

You raised the radar reflector idea, and it is the right instinct — it removes cloud, darkness and the entire discrimination problem in one stroke, because a corner reflector is not merely bright, it is anomalously bright in a way nothing agricultural is. A trihedral of inner leg a returns σ = 4πa⁴/3λ². Requiring 15 dB over farmland clutter in one resolution cell:

The X-band result is the striking one: a 16 cm trihedral weighing under 40 grams is theoretically sufficient against a 1 m spotlight cell. Build it at 25–30 cm for margin against off-boresight loss, incidence-angle variation and imperfect resting attitude, and it is still around 100 g on a 3 kg payload. Arrange eight trihedrals as an octahedral cluster so that whatever attitude the payload settles into, one corner faces up and out. Sentinel-1 is free but needs a 0.69 m, 660 g reflector to beat its coarse cell — a poor trade against a commercial X-band tasking.

Two cheaper things are worth adding alongside, since neither depends on anyone else's spacecraft: a satellite tracker with an independent power budget that survives landing and keeps reporting from the ground, and a RECCO reflector, which weighs 4 grams and lets a mountain-rescue detector find the payload from a helicopter at short range. The corner reflector is elegant; the tracker is what actually recovers payloads.

Caveats

Where this analysis could be wrong

  • Confuser densities are estimates. The candidate counts are order-of-magnitude figures for Meseta farmland, not measurements. The ranking of strategies is robust; the absolute numbers could be off by a factor of several.
  • The sphere may not be intact, exposed, or still there. It could have fragmented on impact, lodged in a tree or hedge, been partly buried, or been picked up by a farmer within days. A half-obscured sphere still yields a workable signature, but a recovered-and-removed one yields nothing.
  • Prices are indicative and drawn from public reseller listings rather than a quote. Treat them as the shape of the commercial structure, not as figures to budget against.
  • Everything assumes a reasonably flat, open landing site. Under canopy or against a north-facing slope in winter, the whole optical approach degrades sharply and the drone or SAR routes become the only viable ones.
