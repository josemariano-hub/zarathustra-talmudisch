The target

A sphere is not a white panel

The instinct is to treat a 70 cm white porexpan ball as a 0.385 m² patch of albedo 0.80. It is not. A Lambertian sphere scatters flux across a curved surface, and only part of that curvature is simultaneously lit and facing the sensor. Integrating the lit-and-visible cap gives an apparent reflectance over the sphere's projected footprint of

ρeff
0.53 – 0.59   (sun high → sun low)
vs. flat panel
67% – 73% of the material albedo
Footprint
0.385 m²  =  4.3 px at 30 cm, 1.5 px at 50 cm

So you lose about a quarter of the brightness you might have budgeted for. It still does not matter much — against dry Meseta soil at ρ ≈ 0.20 the surface contrast is roughly 0.39 reflectance units, and about 0.24 after atmospheric attenuation. That is an enormous signal. Sensor noise is nowhere near the limiting factor: Pléiades Neo's noise-equivalent contrast is around 0.0013, some 180× smaller than the signal.

The shadow is the bigger target

A sphere sitting on soil stands 0.70 m proud of the ground, and for most of the year in central Spain its shadow presents more pixels than the sphere itself. At a typical 10:30 local-solar overpass at latitude 40.4°N:

Operational consequence

Prefer a low sun elevation acquisition, not a high one. The usual instinct is to want the brightest, most overhead illumination; here the opposite is true. A low sun roughly triples the signature and adds a directional bright–dark dipole that is far more distinctive than a bright dot alone.

In practice you cannot choose: the 30 cm optical constellations fly sun-synchronous orbits with a mid-morning descending node, so sun elevation is set by your date, not by your order. For an August landing imaged promptly, that means about 57° and a 0.46 m shadow — the weakest geometry of the year.

This flight

That is still a detection, but it is no longer a description, and description is what rejects bales. In August the case for 30 cm over 50 cm is stronger than it would be in winter, not weaker.

Time pressure

Cereal harvest across the Meseta finishes through July, and stubble ploughing runs from August into September. A sphere lying in a stubble field has a genuinely limited window before it is buried, shredded, or pushed into a field margin. This argues for accepting a good-enough acquisition now over an optimal one later, and it is worth saying plainly to both providers — a deadline that is real makes a goodwill request easier to prioritise, not harder.

Two things to take from this. First, Pléiades Neo is the right instrument — at 30 cm the sphere is a genuine multi-pixel object with a resolvable shadow, not a single hot pixel, and its six bands give you a little spectral leverage on top. Second, PlanetScope will not find it. PlanetScope is still worth pulling, just for a different job: masking the search area down to plausible terrain, confirming which fields were harvested or ploughed between the flight and the acquisition, and checking cloud state on candidate dates. Use it to shrink and schedule, not to detect.

The actual problem

Discrimination, not detection

Central Spain is full of bright compact objects around a metre across: white-wrapped silage bales, limestone float, plastic sheeting, agricultural debris, and a great many sheep. A single image gives you thousands of candidates in a 50 km² box. The filters that actually work are temporal.

The archive baseline is what turns this from impossible into a morning's work. Anything already present before the flight — every rock, every permanent structure — subtracts away. Two post-landing dates then kill everything that moves. What survives is a short list of small, bright, sun-aligned dipoles that appeared in your window and stayed put, which is a very specific description of your payload.

The one that bites

Round silage bales are the residual problem: white, compact, three-dimensional, they cast a shadow, and they appear in bursts during baling season. Your discriminator is size — a bale is 1.2–1.5 m against your 0.70 m — and that discrimination only exists at 30 cm. At 50 cm a bale and a sphere are both “a bright blob about two pixels across”. This is the single strongest argument for Pléiades Neo over SkySat.

Worth knowing before the conversation: SkySat tasking carries an order minimum around $15,000 and archive around $5,000, with a 25 km² polygon minimum. This is also precisely why the goodwill route is worth pursuing. The marginal cost to Planet or Airbus of one pass over an existing collection corridor is small; the invoice minimum is a commercial floor, not a physical one.

Sequence

How to run the search

  1. Reconstruct the descent before buying anything

    Last telemetry fix, burst altitude, descent rate, and ERA5 winds at the actual flight time, integrated down through the column. Free, and the highest-leverage step available: halving the ellipse halves the candidate list. Tooling for this is written and tested — it takes a telemetry CSV and emits the 50% and 95% ellipses plus a square tasking AOI as GeoJSON. If reanalysis is unavailable it derives the wind profile from the balloon's own ascent track, which is arguably better: it is the actual air the payload flew through.

  2. Check whether someone already photographed it

    Spain's PNOA orthophoto programme flies at 25–50 cm and regional flights over Castilla y León and Castilla-La Mancha are frequent. If a flight passed over the area after the landing date, that imagery is free and already at the resolution you need. Check the IGN archive before spending anything.

  3. Mask the search area

    Use Sentinel-2 and PlanetScope to strip out water, woodland canopy, urban fabric and anything under structure. You are not detecting with these; you are deciding where a white sphere could plausibly be visible from above at all.

  4. Pull the archive baseline

    Latest pre-flight high-resolution coverage of the masked area. Without this the whole approach degrades by an order of magnitude, so secure it before committing to tasking.

  5. Task two post-landing acquisitions

    Separated by at least three days so that anything mobile decorrelates. Low sun elevation preferred.

  6. Run a matched filter, not a threshold

    The template is a bright disc of 0.7 m with a dark ellipse trailing along the known solar azimuth at the known length for that date and time. Both lobes, correct orientation, correct separation, scored in sigma units and stacked with cross-date persistence and novelty against the baseline. This is written and validated: on a synthetic 30 cm scene seeded with 73 confusers — bales, limestone float, sheep, and bales appearing after the baseline — the target ranked first at 22.8σ against a next-best of 3.6.

  7. Ground-truth the shortlist

    A hundred-odd candidates is a day of driving, or an afternoon of drone sorties. Sort by descending match score and by accessibility, and go look.

Worth weighing honestly

A contracted VTOL drone survey covers roughly 45 km²/day at 2–3 cm for about €1,500/day, and sees under partial canopy and into ditches where a satellite never will. Across every realistic ellipse size the two routes cost about the same, so this is a capability choice rather than a budget one — see Flying it ourselves below. They also combine well: satellite to triage the wide area down to a shortlist, drone to close it out.

The boom

A 1.5 m carbon-fibre appendage changes the search

New payload fact: a ~1.5 m carbon-fibre boom connects an Insta360 to the sphere — and carbon fibre is conductive. The payload has been carrying a linear radar scatterer all along. Per channel, honestly:

Radar — bright but aspect-fussy. A conducting cylinder returns σ = 2πL²r/λ at broadside. The confirmed 8 mm tube gives 4.6 m² at 24 GHz (RCS scales linearly with radius; the formula sits at its validity edge at circumference = 2λ, good to a couple of dB, and CF's modest conductivity still reflects with under 1 dB loss). The glint lobe stays 0.24°. The saving geometry holds: a straight survey pass sweeps azimuth and crosses the glint once per side regardless of boom heading — ~65 ms, ~65 chirps, ~+3 dB single-look over clutter at a pulled-in 150 m offset and ~+11 dB across the dwell. And the near-horizontal caveat is now resolved: the boom most probably lies level, and the computed tilt tolerance (~±10°, from the 31–50° depression-angle span across the 100–200 m offset band) comfortably covers it. Channel upgraded from stochastic to reliable: one flash per side per pass at ≤150 m offset, guaranteed by the azimuth sweep; residual risk is burial only. Two rules: transient CFAR (persistence would erase the flash) and line spacing ≤300 m. And the radar co-flies with the day RGB bay — with its power now honestly on the books. The audit caught an omission (every endurance figure had ignored the radar's draw) and forced a better flight config: the DCA1000 capture board (~4–5 W, >100 g) never flies — the IWR1443's on-chip DSP runs range-FFT + CFAR and reports detections over UART, so the flight bay is the module alone at ~70 g and ~2.5 W. Cost of the second channel, flown smart: 9 minutes of endurance (136 → 127 min, −6%); flown naive it would be −16%, rejected. Day sorties fly two deterministic channels at once, cross-cueing: a flash pins a range strip for the line filter, an optical candidate gets a free radar look next line.

Satellite SAR — a lottery ticket, now thinner. Nominal 1.8 m² at X-band, further cut by the resonant thin-wire regime (polarization-dependent), against one fixed look per pass and a 0.6° lobe: ~1% per scene. Never task SAR for it.

Drone RGB — still the best new discriminator, now via a line filter. At 2.9 cm the 8 mm boom is 52 px long but only 0.28 px wide — sub-pixel width dilutes the contrast to ~0.07 per pixel, faint to the eye. But a 52-px correlated streak is exactly what an oriented line filter recovers (√52 ≈ 7× gain along the line), and the 2.4-px Insta360 blob anchors its far end. Keep the compound sphere+line+blob template with the line as a supporting cue — and on the 50 m inspection pass it becomes a 125 px × 0.66 px feature: unmistakable. (Satellite: forget the line. Thermal: invisible.)

And the Insta360 itself is no detection channel (radios weeks dead) but is the recovery prize: its card holds the descent video — the ground truth this entire analysis has been reconstructing from physics.

Spectral signature

Diagnostic in the SWIR, unreachable from orbit

Porexpan does have a particular signature, and it is a strong one. Expanded polystyrene carries C–H stretch overtones that cut deep absorption features into the short-wave infrared, around 1200, 1700 and 2300 nm. Against a NIR-to-2300 nm continuum the 1730 nm feature is a 46% band depth for porexpan, where limestone shows none at all. Spectrally this is not a close call — a single SWIR spectrum would identify your sphere beyond argument.

The problem is sampling it. There is no orbital SWIR sensor that resolves a 0.7 m object:

A second reason for Pléiades Neo

The gate leans on the blue end, because natural bright materials all fall off toward the blue and polymers do not. Pléiades Neo's 400–450 nm deep-blue band widens the separation from limestone by about 35% relative to a conventional 4-band sensor whose blue starts at 450 nm. Its six bands were already a modest bonus; with the spectral gate in play they become a second independent argument for that sensor over any 4-band alternative.

Flying it ourselves

The legal ceiling, not the camera, sets the pace

Your instinct about fixed wing is right, with one refinement: VTOL fixed wing, so you can launch and recover vertically from a stubble field with no runway and no hand-launch.

The first surprise is that camera choice barely matters. EASA and AESA cap you at 120 m AGL, which fixes ground sampling by optics alone — and every serious survey payload lands between 1.5 and 3.2 cm there. You need roughly six pixels across the sphere for a confident call; all of them deliver twenty to forty-six. Buying a sharper camera buys nothing. What matters is swath × endurance.

The pre-dawn thermal trick

Porexpan is a near-perfect insulator: thermal inertia around 30 SI against 580 for dry soil and 1,650 for limestone. With almost no thermal ballast it radiates to the night sky and settles at roughly 5 K colder than the surrounding ground before dawn. A 50 mK thermal camera sees that at about 100σ — and critically, it is a signature no bale, rock or plastic sheet can imitate, because they all carry far more heat.

The window is also far wider than a dawn slot. Porexpan reaches its steady cold offset within about half an hour of sunset while soil keeps drawing heat from depth all night, so the contrast stays open for hours. Allowing three hours after sunset for rocks and bales to shed their daytime heat — which is what makes the anomaly clean rather than merely present — August still leaves roughly 7.4 usable hours per night, rising past 11 by December.

The real limit is the detector: 640 pixels where the survey camera has 8,000. Even across a full night thermal sweeps single-digit km². Cereal stubble also cools fast and is the one thing that will mimic it.

Checking the thermal optics

Does diffraction eat the wide lens?

Long-wave infrared runs at 10 µm against a 12 µm pixel pitch and a fast f/1.0 lens, which is exactly the regime where the diffraction spot can rival the detector and make a nominal ground sample meaningless. Worth verifying before trusting any of it.

The useful quantity is Q = λF/p. Below 1 the detector limits resolution; near 2 the optics do. The M3T comes out at Q = 0.83: the diffraction FWHM is 10.3 µm against a 12 µm pixel, so the optics are marginally sharper than the detector samples. The Airy pattern's null-to-null diameter is 24.4 µm — about two pixels, which is what makes this look alarming at first glance — but resolution tracks the PSF core, not the first null.

Correction

The resolution figure survives the check, but the reasoning offered for it earlier did not. The RGB analysis set a threshold of six pixels across for confident identification, and the thermal case was then waved through at 4.1 — a criterion quietly relaxed without saying so. The relaxation is defensible, but only because detection and identification are different problems, and that distinction should have been stated rather than buried.

Detection is contrast-limited, not resolution-limited. A 5 K anomaly against a 50 mK detector is unmistakable at two pixels across, so all three cameras detect the sphere with enormous margin. Identification needs about six pixels, and neither Mavic-class camera delivers it. At 4.1 px the sphere is a cold blob. Thermal tells you where to walk; it does not tell you that what you found is your payload rather than a patch of dry straw or a damp hollow.

So the wide-lens argument holds for what thermal is actually for — sweeping a core at night and producing a short list of positions — and the M3T's extra coverage is real. But it reinforces the division of labour rather than softening it: identification comes from the daylight RGB pass at 2–3 cm, or from driving to the coordinates. If you wanted thermal alone to confirm identity you would need the H30T's 1280×1024 detector at 6.1 cm, and that is a €28,000 aircraft to avoid a car journey.

Batteries

Charge time over flight time, plus the term everyone forgets

The instinct is right, but it under-counts. A pack is unavailable for flight + cooldown + charge after each launch, while the aircraft launches again every flight + swap. A DJI hub refuses to charge a pack above about 40 °C, and in an August field in Castilla that is fifteen to twenty minutes of the pack sitting there doing nothing.

Under visual-line-of-sight the pilot works a bubble of roughly 500 m — 0.79 km². At 4.7 km²/h the M3T clears that entire legal area in ten minutes, about a quarter of one battery. Then you pack up and drive to the next field. Under strict VLOS you are relocation-limited, not energy-limited, and the fourth pack buys you nothing at all.

More batteries only pay off once you have bought more range: an observer and an EVLOS arrangement, a bespoke authorisation, or — cheapest and most effective — a second pilot leapfrogging setups in a second vehicle, which doubles throughput outright.

The exception, and it is the one you care about, is the night thermal sortie. There you are re-flying a small known core rather than covering new ground, so there is no relocation and the aircraft can stay up continuously. Ten sorties across a 7.4-hour August night, three packs cycling, and roughly 7.6 km² of thermal core cleared before sunrise. That is the case the battery maths was made for.

Li-ion vs AA Ultimate Lithium (L91) — the chemistry question, settled

A fair question from a balloon operator, since L91 primaries are the standard HAB battery. Solving the pack: the 220 W climb burst against the L91's 2 A per-cell ceiling forces a 10S9P, 90-cell, 1,350 g pack against the Li-ion's 600 g. Full honesty — it would fly longer (299 vs 159 min, 2.4× the usable energy) but it costs €225 per sortie, consumed, burns the entire rechargeable budget by sortie two, and 90 spring-contact cells in a vibrating airframe is a reliability lottery. Not for propulsion, ever.

Where L91s genuinely belong: an independent 2×AA bus for the drone's Iridium beacon (12 days of duty-cycled beaconing, so the crash that kills the main pack cannot kill the thing that reports the crash) and 4×AA on the next balloon tracker, as every HAB tracker flies. The rule in one line: rechargeables spin the propeller, primaries keep the promise. Meanwhile the airframe absorbs any 3S–6S pack of 450–700 g already on the shelf without redesign.

Buy the airframe used. Buy the batteries new.

Lithium packs are the one component where the second-hand saving is not worth taking. You cannot see cycle count or storage abuse in a photograph; a tired pack sags under load and triggers a low-voltage landing over the wrong field; a swollen cell is a fire in the back of the car. DJI packs run roughly 200–400 cycles, and a ten-day search at ten sorties a day is only about 25 cycles per pack — so new packs will outlast several campaigns. The airframe has no equivalent wear item, which is exactly why buying that used is sensible.

The DIY option

What a purpose-built drone would cost — and the clause that caps it

Before any bill of materials, the fact that shapes the whole design space: a privately built aircraft carries no C-class marking, so STS-02 is unavailable to it at any price. A homebuilt flies Open category A3 — VLOS, under 25 kg, 150 m from uninvolved people — or it needs a full SORA authorisation. Building cannot buy range; it can only buy sensors and redundancy cheaply. So the DIY design splits into the two jobs VLOS work actually rewards.

The night sweeper

Iterating the €1,940 day wing into a quiet night aircraft

Scope first: quiet here means acoustically discreet — pre-dawn sorties that wake no village, no livestock, and no dogs. The flashing green night beacon required by the night rules stays on the aircraft: conspicuity to observers and other airspace users is law and safety, not a design variable.

And this class is not speculative. Air Shepherd flew foam fixed wings with thermal cameras, exclusively at night, over African reserves — over 6,000 flight hours of anti-poaching patrol. The hand-launched foam-plus-LWIR form factor is the Raven/Puma lineage, proven for two decades. We are re-implementing a mature concept at Meseta scale, pointed at one styrofoam ball instead of poachers.

Iteration 2 — the airframe swap is where quiet comes from

Propeller noise scales roughly with tip speed to the 5.5th power, so tip speed is the acoustic design variable. Swapping the twin-boom for a 2.5 m foam motor-glider with a 14-inch folding prop turning at 2,800 rpm cuts tip speed from 72 to 52 m/s and cruise power from 82 to 47 W. Night air helps everything: denser, calmer, no thermals — the numbers above are conservative after dark.

The legal envelope decides most of this before any link budget: an A3 homebuilt flies VLOS, at most ~2 km from the pilot. A link only earns its mass if it serves a mission the aircraft may actually fly — and the ELRS 868 control link already in the BOM closes that entire envelope at 25 g.

Starlink's home is the ground vehicle, where Zero 2 Infinity already owns the kit and the van genuinely needs the fat pipe: candidate review, PNOA tiles, weather, coordinating two teams.

Iridium: wrong as telemetry, right as insurance

340-byte messages with minutes of latency cannot do C2 or imagery. But as a lost-aircraft beacon — 30 g, ~€260 — it turns a night aircraft down in dark scrub from a morning of searching into a walk. The aircraft hunting a lost payload should not itself become a lost payload; carry it. And the same 30 g module, transmitting one fix per minute through a balloon descent, would have made this entire project unnecessary — the cheapest sentence in this document.

The precedent architecture

Air Shepherd's 6,000 night hours flew exactly one shape: a thin RF link from the aircraft to a control vehicle that is the ops centre — pilots, screens, batteries and uplink on wheels, minimum radio in the air. Copy it: aircraft carries ELRS + Iridium beacon and logs everything onboard; the van carries the Starlink, the power station, and the decisions. The van is the node. The wing is a sensor that comes home.

Lessons from Ukraine — the civilian-applicable ones

  • Links fail; autonomy completes the mission. Any architecture needing a continuous link is fragile. Ours already passes — pre-planned mission, onboard logging, post-flight processing. Never let a design decision make the downlink load-bearing.
  • GNSS is a service, not a fact. Largely irrelevant over the Meseta, and free to hedge: ArduPilot's dead-reckoning brings the aircraft home from 2 km with GPS dark. Enable it, test it once.
  • Cheap-and-many beats exquisite-and-one. A built-and-trimmed spare airframe is ~€275. The fleet that keeps flying after a crash out-searches the fleet that grounds itself.
  • Logistics set the sortie rate. Batteries, chargers and field watts govern coverage — the same law the battery section quantified. The van's power budget is mission infrastructure.
  • The BOM's cheapness is the war's supply chain. ELRS links, Pixhawk clones and Li-ion at these prices exist because that ecosystem scaled to wartime volume. Order spares while it is glutted.

Decision: the SIM card is the primary telemetry

LTE promoted from thumbnail path to main link — and the numbers endorse it. MAVLink at 4 Hz plus a 1 fps thermal preview totals 330 kbps sustained against a rural uplink of 5–20 Mbps; a full 7.4-hour August night moves 1.1 GB, which a €10–15/month IoT SIM shrugs at. And it adds the one thing the RF link never had: live thermal preview to the van, so the operator marks hotspots during the sortie rather than after it.

Correction — day is the primary search, not night

A fair challenge exposed a drift in this brief's emphasis: we do not see the sphere better at night — we see it differently, and we get to keep searching after sunset. Sensor for sensor, day RGB wins nearly everything: 2.9 cm against 15.8 cm, 24 px against 4.4, identification in the same pass instead of a descent, 8.9 km² per sortie against 5.9, and no terrain-floor risk. At 3 cm a bale, a stone and a plastic sheet are obviously not a sphere — the satellite's discrimination problem does not exist at drone GSD.

What night genuinely buys: hours (~16 km² of thermal added to ~54 km² of daylight RGB per August 24 h, airtime-limited figures), and the one thing day cannot — the 5 K cold-anomaly signature that no bale, rock or sheet can imitate, which makes thermal the tie-breaker for candidates day RGB cannot call: a white blob deep in a hedge, half-buried, in shadow.

Corrected doctrine: day RGB sweeps; night thermal is the second shift and the discriminator. Procurement re-orders accordingly: airframe + camera + comms first (~€2,640) and start flying days immediately; add the €900 thermal core only when the ellipse exceeds about two day-searches of area or day sorties produce ambiguous candidates. The telemetry still decides everything — a small ellipse never needs the night shift.

Cruise speed, optimized — not assumed

Another fair challenge: 13 m/s was an airframe-class habit, not an optimum. Checked properly: no sensor limits speed (blur 0.35 px at 20 m/s, gapless overlap to 65–90 m/s, thermal smear acceptable), so the optimum depends on which resource is scarce. Energy-limited (few packs, remote setup): ~14 m/s in calm air, rising with wind — the rule of thumb is add half the wind to the airspeed, since the broad energy optimum makes speeding up nearly free in km²/Wh while recovering ~15% of km²/h that ground-speed sag steals. Time-limited (three packs cycling, van charging — the actual day-sweep situation): fly fast, 16–18 m/s, for +30–38% coverage per hour. Night thermal flips back to the energy optimum. Survey lines fly crosswind so crab and sidelap stay uniform, and noise never vetoes: even 20 m/s stays under the 32 dBA night ambient.

The props follow from the two regimes: Aeronaut CAM folding system, ~€135 — 14×8 blades for the quiet night/energy cruise (54 m/s tip), 14×11 for the fast day sweep (61 m/s tip), one 42 mm spinner/yoke, and a magnetic balancer, because an unbalanced folder is the loudest thing on the airframe and eats the thermal camera's vibration budget. Two blade sets, two missions, one aircraft — the same doctrine as the payload bays. Never printed.

The workshop advantage: a Bambu X1C on the team

The airframe's brackets, bays and mounts stop being purchases: CF-PETG for the swappable payload bays, battery tray, motor-mount plate and launch grip; TPU for the thermal core's vibration-isolation mount (better than the silicone hack) and belly skids for stubble landings; PETG for hatches and — importantly — all antenna mounts, since carbon fill is conductive and stays away from RF. Never print props or the wing spar. Net effect: ~€120 of bought hardware becomes ~€25 of filament, every field breakage becomes an overnight reprint — and the same printer manufactures the octahedral corner-reflector frame for the next balloon flight, the part that makes the next recovery a non-event.

Sortie arithmetic, and the sensor worth investing in

A full night sortie: 10 min preflight, 1 min launch and climb, 2 min transit, 128 min on survey lines, 5 min return and land — ~158 min airborne, 5.9 km² scanned per flight, ~16 km² per August night across two-and-a-bit sorties. By day the APS-C covers 8.9 km² in a 138-minute flight.

The upgrade axis that matters is resolution, not sensitivity — NETD upgrades buy nothing at ~100σ margin. A 1280×1024 LWIR core behind a sharp lens (~€3,300) would put 8.8 px on the sphere from cruise, past the 6 px identification threshold. But there is a cheaper zoom lens: altitude.

Descend to inspect — the revised call

The €900 640 core at 50 m AGL gives 6.6 cm GSD and 10.6 px on the sphere — the same identification certainty the expensive core delivers from 120 m. An inspection costs 23 s of spiral descent, a 20 s pass, and a 23 s climb: 1.1 minutes and 0.7 Wh, still under the night ambient at ~26 dBA. Even at 3 false alarms per km² the inspections eat only 13% of the sortie's survey time.

So the profile is: detect at 120 m, geotag, finish the survey lines, then fly one 50 m inspection tour of the queue before landing — and the LTE preview makes the queue live, with the van marking candidates as they appear. The first sortie measures the real false-alarm density; buy the 1280 core only if it comes in well above ~5/km². The €3,300 stays in the pocket until data, not a datasheet, says otherwise.

One safety line: 50 m at night demands a hard terrain floor — flat llanura only, terrain data loaded, inspection waypoints altitude-capped, no descents in barrancos. The sphere is not worth the aircraft.

Our own radar

€550 turns the next flight's reflector into a 12 km²/h beacon

Once the next payload carries the printed corner-reflector cluster, nobody's satellite is needed — the sweeper grows a radar bay. The sane DIY path is not microwave engineering: it is a COTS 24 GHz FMCW front-end (ISM band, licence-free at the 100 mW EIRP the modules ship with) plus your own signal processing on the Pi.

Picking our own wavelength transforms the target. At 24 GHz a trihedral with just 15 cm legs returns 13.7 m² — a truck-sized radar echo folded from just ~63 g — three interlocking foil-on-Depron plates in a printed slot-joint frame (the octahedral geometry shares every face among its 8 corners, and at 24 GHz 12 µm kitchen foil is 23 skin depths: a perfect reflector at 32 g/m²), while the porexpan itself is invisible (~0.001 m²). The radar equation closes at 227 m noise-limited range; at a 200 m side-looking operating range the reflector sits 6 dB over stubble clutter before integration and ~16 dB after. Sweeping both sides gives a ~340 m swath and 12 km²/h — more than double the day camera — indifferent to cloud, fog, darkness, and the sphere being under a hedge, with 0.75 m range resolution localising the hit to a strip a tractor can drive down.

Caveats

Where this analysis could be wrong

  • Confuser densities are estimates. The candidate counts are order-of-magnitude figures for Meseta farmland, not measurements. The ranking of strategies is robust; the absolute numbers could be off by a factor of several.
  • The sphere may not be intact, exposed, or still there. It could have fragmented on impact, lodged in a tree or hedge, been partly buried, or been picked up by a farmer within days. A half-obscured sphere still yields a workable signature, but a recovered-and-removed one yields nothing.
  • Prices are indicative and drawn from public reseller listings rather than a quote. Treat them as the shape of the commercial structure, not as figures to budget against.
  • Everything assumes a reasonably flat, open landing site. Under canopy or against a north-facing slope in winter, the whole optical approach degrades sharply and the drone or SAR routes become the only viable ones.
  • The spectral values are library figures, not measurements. Before relying on the flat-and-bright gate, put a field spectrometer on an offcut of the actual porexpan you flew — a ten-minute measurement pins the gate to your material instead of to a literature average, and would be worth doing regardless.
  • Drone coverage figures assume good conditions. Wind above roughly 12 m/s, thermals over stubble in the afternoon, and airspace restrictions near aerodromes or Natura 2000 sites will all cut the daily figure. Treat 45 km²/day as a planning number, not a guarantee.
